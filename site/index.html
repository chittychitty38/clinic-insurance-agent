Built for a phone call, not a chat window

No dead air while the insurer thinks.

The slow part of every call is the insurer, two to ten seconds. Riley asks about the card on the patient’s chart the moment it finds them, so the answer is waiting when the caller finishes reading it. The early answer is only used if Riley would have sent the exact same request.

Front desk standardsRiley · v1
  • Gets the member ID right on a bad lineB and D, M and N, five and nine sound alike on a phone. If what Riley heard is one or two of those slips away from the card on the chart, it uses the chart card and logs what it heard.
  • Riley ignores “mm-hmm” and stops for “wait”Acknowledgements never cut Riley off mid-copay. “Wait,” “hold on” and “no” stop it instantly. It also waits longer after numbers, so you can finish an ID.
  • Patient while you find your cardTwelve quiet seconds gets “Take your time, I’m still here,” never a hang-up. Krisp noise suppression cleans up calls from buses, dorms and dining halls.
  • Eye emergencies before insuranceVision loss, a curtain over the vision, new flashes and floaters, chemicals or injuries go straight to 911 or the ER. A painful red eye in a contact lens wearer gets an urgent callback at the top of the staff queue.
  • Honest from the first sentenceThe greeting says the line is recorded (California needs every party’s consent) and that Riley is a virtual assistant. Ask for a person and you get a callback.
  • Every call graded after it hangs upPass or fail against the rules that matter: no number that didn’t come from a tool, emergencies first, nobody left without a next step. Staff review the failures, not every call.
The decision that matters

The language model never decides a fact.

An insurer’s answer can list 40 benefit lines. Asking a model to pick the right copay out of that, live on a call, is how a patient gets quoted the wrong number. So the work is split four ways, and each part can only fail in its own way.

Campus Eye Center

accepted = our contract with the insurer × coverage active today

Eligibility isn’t acceptance. A Kaiser member with active coverage still isn’t covered here, and a UnitedHealthcare member can be on a plan whose network leaves the clinic out. Riley only says yes when both are true, and explains which one isn’t.

Which copay? Read from the topEye doctors bill as specialists
  1. Specialist office visitService type 98 or BY, labeled specialist, in network, at the office
  2. Any office visitSame, without the label. Never a primary-care-only copay
  3. Plan-level copayService type 30, when nothing more specific exists
  4. Two amounts left? Staff confirmsRiley names both and books a callback. It never picks one

What real insurer data taught us

The first live run passed 16 of 28, and every miss became a rule.

Documentation describes the 271. Real responses are messier. Each of these would have put a wrong answer in a patient’s ear.

16/2828/28Live sandbox runs, before and after
PROVIDER IS OUT NETWORK FOR MEMBER

We take UnitedHealthcare. Her plan doesn’t take us.

A naive agent says “you’re covered.” Riley says her specific plan lists the office as out of network.

QUALIFIED_MEDICARE_BENEFICIARY

The Medicare patient who owes $0

0% coinsurance looked like a bug until the insurance type explained it: Medicaid pays her Medicare cost-sharing.

SPECIALIST, PAR NETWORK

Anthem lists two specialist copays

$30 on the EPO network, $45 on PAR. The 271 can’t say which network the clinic is in, so that’s clinic config. Riley quotes $45.

coverageLevel: FAMILY

Aetna’s copay hid behind a label

The $15 specialist copay is tagged FAMILY. That describes the plan, not the amount. Filtering it out silently lost the copay.

place of service 99

Cigna’s $10 trap

The $10 copays were telehealth-vendor and “client specific network” lines. The real specialist copay is $20, filed under place of service “other.”

payer ID 62308

A dental card with a medical payer ID

Cigna dental shares Cigna medical’s payer ID, so dental-only plans are spotted from the plan itself, and Riley asks for the medical card.

planDateInformation

Active, with 2024 plan dates

Sandbox responses are frozen in time. In production, “active” with an expired plan date is a contradiction, and Riley hands it to a person.

HTTP 429 + AAA 42

Errors don’t always look like errors

An insurer outage can arrive as a rate-limit status. The client reads the 271’s error codes, not the HTTP status, and retries once.

When the answer isn’t yes

No dead ends, and no guessing to avoid one.

The calls that matter most are the hard ones.

Not accepted

  • Confirms the coverage is real and active, so the caller knows their insurance works, just not here.
  • Explains why in one sentence: Kaiser plans only cover Kaiser doctors.
  • Offers the $175 self-pay exam, a callback, or the member-services route to an in-network doctor.
  • Never quotes an insurer’s dollar amount on a plan the clinic doesn’t take. There’s a test for that.

When the insurer pushes back

  • AAA 42 insurer down: one automatic retry, then a callback.
  • AAA 72 · 73 · 75 ID, name or member not found: asks once to re-read the card or for the policyholder, then a callback.
  • AAA 43 · 79 the clinic’s own setup rejected: tells the caller it isn’t their fault and flags it for staff.
  • Inactive coverage: asks for a newer card before anything else.

Roadmap

What it needs before real patients.

This build proves the hard part: turning an insurer’s response into a sentence that’s right. Production is mostly integration and compliance.

  1. Verify identity

    Name, date of birth and caller ID before discussing anything protected. The brief waived it for this version.

  2. HIPAA end to end

    BAAs with Vapi (HIPAA mode), Stedi, the model provider and hosting. The call log moves to an encrypted database.

  3. The real chart

    Patients and insurance on file read from the practice-management system, like ModMed or Nextech, instead of a demo list.

  4. Richer checks

    Office-visit (98) and vision (AL) service types alongside 30, VSP and EyeMed for routine exams, Medicare number lookup.

  5. Network per plan

    Contract status per product, not per insurer, since one insurer sells plans on several networks.

  6. Spanish

    A bilingual line with reviewed Spanish scripts, so numbers are never left to translation.
